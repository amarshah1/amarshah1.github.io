I (somewhat) recently applied to PhD programs in Computer Science. There are a
lot of really [good
resources](https://jedyang.com/post/list-of-awesome-cs-phd-application-advice/)
that talk about PhD applications. I don't hope to replace this depth of
material. I'll try to focus on my own specific experience and hopefully this can
be useful for people in similar situations.

Many blog posts on PhD applications either provide a very high-level summary of
PhD applications or they treat the application process like a game, where you
have to optimize everything about your application in order to get into the best
possible school. I think the former can be unhelpful and the latter can create
the wrong overall atmosphere around PhD programs. I think its most helpful to
view the application process as trying to find the best match both between
students and professors/schools. You can try to represent yourself as positively
as possible  where each party tries to provide the necessary
information to make the correct match.

In this framework, there are essentially four rounds:

1. The professors/universities provide information to the students about their
   program/lab.
2. Students pick which universities to apply to based off of this information.
   They provide these schools with information (and money) via their
   applications.
3. Based on this information, schools will accept a certain number of students.
4. Finally, students who have gotten into multiple schools will pick one school
   to attend.

The next few sections will describe my advice at each of these stages.

## Stage 0: Everything Before

How do you know a CS PhD program is right for you? The goal of such a program is
to train you to produce new knowledge in computer science.

This is very different than what you do as an undergraduate. It can be a lot
more fun in some ways, but it can be a lot more punishing as well.

The best way to know if this is something you will enjoy is to get your hands
dirty and do some research. Graduate schools are also looking for students who
have done research before because they know that they have the skills and more
importantly the temperament to do research.

To get started look at professors at your undergraduate university and see if
any of their research looks interesting. If so, you could reach out.
Alternatively, take a graduate level class, usually these are project-based and
extensions to the project could be turned into research. There are also [summer
REU
programs](https://new.nsf.gov/funding/initiatives/reu/search?f%5B0%5D=reu_research_area:25737)
you can apply to. Usually these are funded and you get paid.

## Stage 1: Where to apply?

When applying to graduate school, you should have some idea of what area you
want to work in. For instance, I applied for Programming Languages and Formal
Methods. You can change this later, but you should have some sense of what you
like.

After that, websites like [CSRankings](https://csrankings.org) can be useful for
narrowing down a list of schools. The website is deeply imperfect, but it can be
useful in producing an initial list. I would ask the
professors or PhD students if they have recommendations. They have the
best sense of what work other research groups are doing.

## Stage 2:  Apply

There are a few main things you need to apply to graduate schools: (1) 3 letters
of recommendation, (2) your CV and (3) a statement of purpose, and (4) your
undergraduate/masters transcript. Schools used to require a GRE score, but this
is incredibly uncommon these days.

Letters of recommendation are probably the most important component of your
application. These letters need to speak to your ability to do research, so at
least one of the letters should come from a professor that you did a research
project under. Ideally, this is someone that should be known by people at the
schools you are applying to. However, this is not always possible, especially if
you are applying to schools from a different country. Not all three letters need
to come from professors you have done research under if three such professors do
not exist. Other letters can come from professors whose graduate class you took
(especially if there was a large project component to the class), professors who
you TA'd for, or even a manager from an internship.

Your CV, as a record of things you have done, is important. It should first and
foremost highlight your research projects. You should have ``education'' and
``research projects'' sections at the top. After that, you can have sections for
publications, presentations, posters, student research
competitions, or anything similar close to the top. Then you can also include
sections for teaching experience, industrial internships, awards, academic
service, etc. lower down. Most CVs are like this and you can check out mine as a
reference.

The statement of purpose is important and there are a lots of [examples
online](https://cs-sop.notion.site/). The main failure mode during statement of
purpose writing is to include irrelevant anecdotes—this is not like writing a
college essay. The SoP should answer two questions: *why do you want to do
research* and *why are you qualified to do research full time*. Ideally you
should try to create a coherent narrative about the work that you have done and
what you want to do. You should be specific enough that you show that you know
what you are talking about, but broad enough that you can appeal to several
professors without putting yourself in a box. You should list a few professors
in your last paragraph or two and describe what about their research appeals to
you. As another example, you can read  [my SoP](/files/Research_Statement.pdf).
There are a lot of things I like about it, but a few things I would have done
differently.

I would try to avoid stressing too much about your transcript—this is the least
important of everthing I have mentioned. By the time you are seriously thinking
about applying to PhD programs its probably too late to do much about it.
Programs mostly look for a signal that you are capable of doing research in
computer science and research skills are not strongly correlated with
undergraduate grades. The most important thing to demonstrate with your
transcript is that you have sufficiently challenged yourself with a difficult
courseload and that you did not do terribly (i.e. failed all your classes).


## Stage 3:  Schools decide

After you apply the schools pick who is admitted. Schools may say that admission
is done via committee, but that is not completely the case. Individual professors
will see your applications (especially the professors whose names you listed). They
may reach out to interview you (but they may not, so don't freak out if you don't hear
back about an interview).

## Stage 4: You decide

If you have made it through the first three stages and you get into schools,
they should pay for you to visit them during ``official visit days''. Now that
you have cleared the bar, schools really want you to attend. They will *wine and
dine* you. When you talk to professors and graduate students at the visit days
try to get straight answers to your question. Then after visiting all of the
schools try to make a rational decision based on what is important to you. There
are probably two pieces of advice I would give you:

1. Your advisor *matters a ton*
2. Everything other than your advisor *matters a ton*

For the first point, the relationship between you and your advisor has an
enormous impact on your PhD. Your advisor is responsible for helping you pick
research problems, providing guidance on your research, turning your work into
actual papers, and advocating for you in the department and to the broader
scientific community. Hence, your advisor matters a lot—they need to be kind and
understanding, but also capable, organized and have *good ideas* that you are
excited about.

However, the second point is to emphasize that you should not just go to the
school with the best possible advisor. You can have the greatest advisor in the
world and still be unhappy or unsuccessful. It is also important to consider the
geographical location, the rest of the department, the other graduate students
in the research group, the physical space you will be working in, etc. It is
easy to fall in love with the *platonic ideal of a PhD*, working with some of
the greatest minds in a field to solve the largest questions. This is very
different from what the day-to-day looks like and its important to consider this
when choosing where to go.
